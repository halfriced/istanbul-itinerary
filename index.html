Four days in Istanbul

Our hotel is on the old Hippodrome, looking straight at the Blue Mosque, so most of the old city is a short walk from the door. The plan tries to show the city as it really is: Byzantine Constantinople and Ottoman Istanbul built on top of each other, the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn running through it, neighbourhoods worth wandering on both continents, and a lot of good food. Meat in Türkiye is halal as a rule, so we can eat almost anywhere; we skip only bars, meyhanes and places built around alcohol or pork.

The Hippodrome and the Great Palace mosaics

Outside our door · free · the Mosaic Museum behind the Blue Mosque is about €10 and takes 30 minutes

The long open square in front of our hotel was the Hippodrome of Constantinople, a chariot-racing stadium for 100,000 people and for a thousand years the city's public square, where emperors were cheered and riots started. The track ran where the gardens are now. Three monuments stand along its spine: the Obelisk of Theodosius, carved in Egypt around 1450 BC and brought here in 390; the bronze Serpent Column, made in 479 BC to celebrate the Greek victory over Persia and moved from Delphi; and the rough stone Walled Obelisk. Behind the Blue Mosque, the Great Palace Mosaic Museum shows a floor from the emperors' palace, full of hunting scenes and animals.

Tickets. The Hippodrome is a public square. The Mosaic Museum sells tickets at its door, by card (also on muze.gov.tr).

Hagia Sophia (Ayasofya)

650 m, 8 minutes' walk · free for prayer · the tourist gallery costs €25

Justinian's great church, finished in 537, was the largest interior in the world for almost a thousand years. It became a mosque in 1453, a museum in 1934 and a mosque again in 2020. This year its famous dome, which seems to float on a ring of windows, is out of sight. A major restoration fills the hall with scaffolding, and in mid-September the work moved on to the eastern half-dome. Prayers carry on throughout. We'll go once, for Asr on Thursday on our way to the boat (or Fajr on Sunday if we land late), simply to pray in the place.

Good to know: worshippers enter free through the gate on the park side, which opens a little before each call to prayer. There are no official online tickets, so any website selling them is a reseller.

Tickets. None needed to pray: worshippers go in free through their own gate on the park side, which opens shortly before each call to prayer, and pray on the ground floor.

A proper visit, if we want one. The tourist route is the upper gallery only, 08:00–19:00 (on Fridays it's closed 12:00–14:30). Tickets are €25 (about ₺1,350) each and are sold only at the ticket office by the entrance; the one official online option is a combined ticket with the Hagia Sophia History and Experience Museum next door, at booking.demmuseums.com. With the dome hidden by scaffolding this year, go with a guide or the audio guide, or it's mostly scaffolding. Allow 45 minutes. Where it fits: Friday 08:00, before Topkapı (breakfast at 07:30 instead); Friday 15:15 after the Cistern, taking time from the bazaar; or Sunday morning for the two staying on.

The Blue Mosque (Sultan Ahmed Camii)

200 m, 3 minutes' walk · free · our local mosque for the trip

Built between 1609 and 1616 as the empire's answer to Hagia Sophia, it's the only Ottoman mosque with six minarets. It takes its nickname from the twenty thousand İznik tiles on its upper walls. A long restoration finished in 2023, so the dome and tiles are fully on show again. As worshippers we skip the tourist queue and go in through the courtyard.

Good to know: for Friday prayer, be inside by 12:30. Tourists are kept out on Fridays until about 14:30, which doesn't affect us.

A proper look, beyond Jummah. Free, no ticket, through the visitors' entrance on the south side. Visitors are let in between prayers, roughly 08:30–12:15, 13:45–16:30 and 17:30–18:30 (the windows shift with prayer times), and on Fridays only from about 14:30. Quietest 08:30–10:00; allow 30–45 minutes; scarves are lent at the door. Where it fits: we're inside for Jummah anyway, but for a slow daytime look at the tiles, Friday 14:15 after lunch (it's next to the Cistern), or Sunday morning for the two staying on. A small, beautiful mosque by Sinan, finished in 1571–72, with some of the finest İznik tiles in the city. It's on our list for four fragments of the Black Stone of the Kaaba, set in gold frames: above the entrance, high on the mihrab, above the door of the minbar and under its small conical roof. Tradition says they came to Istanbul in the reign of Süleyman the Magnificent.

Good to know: if it looks closed, it often isn't. Lift the heavy leather curtain at the door and go in, or come at prayer time. The fragments are set out of reach, and the Istanbul mufti asks visitors not to touch or kiss them.

Topkapı Palace

1.2 km, 15 minutes' walk · ₺2,750 each, including the Harem and Hagia Irene · 09:00–17:00 (last tickets) · closed Tuesdays · cards only

For nearly four hundred years this was the home and court of the Ottoman sultans. It isn't one building but a walled world of courtyards, pavilions, kitchens and gardens. The Harem was the family's private quarters, and more of it opened to visitors this year, including the concubines' courtyard with its hamam and the Kuşhane kitchen. The Treasury holds the Spoonmaker's Diamond and the Topkapı Dagger. In the Sacred Relics chamber, where the Qur'an is recited without a break, are the Prophet's ﷺ mantle, sword and bow, a footprint and hairs from his beard, alongside the swords of the Companions. From the terraces of the fourth courtyard you look straight down the Bosphorus.

Good to know: the audio guide is all we need. Dress modestly for the Relics chamber, where, as in the Treasury, photos aren't allowed. Skip-the-line tickets don't skip the security check, so arrive by 08:45. Konyalı, the palace's long-running restaurant, left its old building for restoration. Google Maps now lists it in the old guardhouse in the outer courtyard, open 09:00–17:30, but we haven't confirmed it's serving.

Tickets. Topkapı is run by the National Palaces administration, and its only official online shop is millisaraylar.gov.tr ("Bilet Al"). It's ₺2,750 each, including the Harem and Hagia Irene; the e-ticket has no time slot and is valid for a single visit on any day within 90 days, so we just turn up at 09:00. The Basilica Cistern (Yerebatan Sarnıcı)

550 m, 7 minutes' walk · daily from 09:00 · we go at 14:30 on Friday · cards or İstanbulkart only

A sixth-century underground reservoir that once stored water for the Great Palace above. There are 336 marble columns standing in rows in the half-dark, and two carved Medusa heads, one on its side and one upside down, reused as column bases. It stays around 14°C all year. It looks the same by day as by night, since no daylight reaches it; the difference is the crowd, which peaks in the early afternoon. 45 minutes is plenty.

Good to know: in June the city handed the Cistern over to the national foundations directorate, and two days later a court froze the handover. Since then, reports disagree about who runs it and what it costs. The last clear prices for foreign visitors were ₺1,950 by day and ₺3,000 in the evening. The doors close from 18:30 to 19:30 between sessions.

Tickets. No booking: buy at the door, by card or İstanbulkart, no cash. If the queue is longer than about 20 minutes, go to the smaller Şerefiye Cistern (the Cistern of Theodosius) five minutes away instead: 10:00–22:00, ₺900, a light show, no queue, 4.4 on Google from 11,000 reviews.

Kadıköy

Fener and Balat

On the Golden Horn · Saturday's ferry lands at Fener · the T5 tram stops at Fener and Balat · free · allow 2½–3 hours

Fener was the Greek quarter and Balat the Jewish one, and the two run into each other along the Golden Horn. There are steep cobbled lanes of narrow painted houses, old churches and synagogues, the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate, and the red-brick Greek school at the top of the hill. In the last ten years the area has filled with cafés and antique shops without losing its neighbourhood feel, and these are the most photogenic streets in the city. Chora (Kariye) and the land walls

A Saturday option · 15–20 minutes uphill from Balat · €20 · Saturday–Thursday 09:00–18:00, not open to tourists on Fridays · 45–60 minutes

A small Byzantine church whose mosaics and frescoes, finished around 1321, are the finest in Istanbul and among the finest anywhere: the life of Christ and of Mary in gold mosaic in the two entrance halls, and in the side chapel a fresco of the Resurrection that is one of the great paintings of the Middle Ages. It became a mosque after the conquest, a museum in 1945 and a mosque again in 2024; tourists now see the mosaic halls on a separate paid route. Five minutes away, the Tekfur Palace, the last surviving Byzantine palace in the city, stands against the Theodosian land walls, which ran 6.5 km from the Golden Horn to the Sea of Marmara and held for a thousand years until 1453.

Good to know: visits pause for fifteen minutes before each prayer, so avoid arriving just before Asr. From here, a taxi to Eyüp takes about ten minutes.

Tickets. At the booth by the tourist entrance, €20 each by card. The MuseumPass isn't valid here, and there's no need to book.

Ayvansaray and the graves of the Companions

Between Balat and Eyüp, where the old land walls reach the Golden Horn · T5 stop Ayvansaray · free · about 30 minutes

Tradition holds that many of the Muslim soldiers who besieged Constantinople in the 670s are buried around here, a short way before Eyüp, and that Companions of the Prophet ﷺ were among them. We make two stops. Atik Mustafa Paşa Mosque, a Byzantine church that became a mosque and is known locally as the Câbir Mosque, keeps a tomb attributed to Câbir, a Companion said to have fallen nearby in 678. Under the Haliç Bridge, the Sahabeler Haziresi, the Companions' burial ground, has graves attributed to Abu Shayba al-Khudri, by tradition the Prophet's ﷺ milk-brother, and to Hamdullah and Ahmad al-Ansari. It's a quiet, local place, and a fitting way to arrive at Eyüp.

Good to know: these are traditional attributions rather than documented burials. It's a residential area, so dress modestly and keep voices down.

Eyüp Sultan Mosque, the shrine of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari (RA)

On the Golden Horn · T5 to Eyüpsultan Teleferik · free · the tomb is open until the evening prayer

The holiest Islamic site in Istanbul. Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, the Companion who hosted the Prophet ﷺ in his home when he arrived in Madinah, died during the Arab siege of Constantinople in the 670s and was buried outside the walls. After the conquest, Mehmed II built a mosque over his grave in 1458, and for centuries every new sultan was girded with the sword of Osman here. The tomb chamber, behind a wall of İznik tiles, is free to enter, and visitors file quietly past. It feels devotional rather than touristy: pilgrims, families, and little boys dressed up for their circumcision celebrations. The hillside above is one of the oldest Muslim cemeteries in the city.

Good to know: the tomb closes briefly at prayer times, so don't arrive right on Asr or Maghrib. Dress modestly. Women need a headscarf, and loaners are available at the entrance. It's busiest on Friday middays.

Üsküdar is the conservative, deeply Ottoman face of the Asian shore. Mihrimah Sultan Mosque, built by Sinan in 1548 for Süleyman's daughter, stands right beside the ferry pier. Lokantas: how they work, and where to try one

Istanbul's everyday home cooking · lunch is best · point at what you want

A lokanta is a canteen of home-style Turkish cooking. There's no menu to read: the day's dishes sit in steel trays behind a glass counter, cooked that morning, and you simply point. It's how Istanbul office workers and shopkeepers eat lunch, it's cheap and fast, and it's the best way to taste proper Turkish food beyond kebabs.

  • How to order: walk up to the counter, point, and say bir porsiyon (a portion) or yarım porsiyon (a half portion, so we can try more). Add pilav (buttery rice) on the side. Sit down; the plates are brought over.
  • When: go between 12:00 and 14:00, when the trays are full and fresh. By late afternoon the best dishes are gone, and many lokantas close by 17:00.
  • Paying: bread comes free, water is cheap, and you pay at the till on the way out. For four, expect €25–45.

What to point at

  • Meat, no vegetables: kuzu tandır (slow-roasted lamb that falls off the bone), tas kebabı (meat stewed until tender in its own gravy), etli nohut (chickpeas with lamb), İzmir köfte (meatballs baked with potatoes), tavuk sote (chicken sauté; it has some peppers).
  • The classic: hünkar beğendi, "the sultan's delight": lamb stew on a smoky, cheesy aubergine purée. The purée is vegetable, but it doesn't taste like it.
  • Lighter: mercimek çorbası (red lentil soup with lemon), tavuk suyu (chicken soup), and pilav.
  • Vegetable dishes (for the others): kuru fasulye (white bean stew, the national lunch), karnıyarık (aubergine stuffed with mince), dolma and sarma (stuffed peppers and vine leaves).
  • Pudding to finish: sütlaç (baked rice pudding), kazandibi (caramelised milk pudding) or ayva tatlısı (poached quince with kaymak).

Getting around, and prayer times

The T1 tram is our main line: Kabataş, Karaköy, Eminönü, Sultanahmet and Beyazıt. Trams come every few minutes from 06:00 until midnight, and the Sultanahmet stop is five minutes from the hotel. Avoid the Eminönü–Sultanahmet stretch between 17:00 and 19:00, when it's packed.

The T5 tram runs along the Golden Horn from Eminönü: Küçükpazar, Cibali, Fener, Balat, Ayvansaray, Feshane and Eyüpsultan Teleferik. It runs from 06:00 to midnight, every five minutes or so at busy times.

Ferries cross from Eminönü and Karaköy to Kadıköy and Üsküdar in 15–20 minutes, and leave every 15–20 minutes. The Golden Horn ferry (the Kadıköy–Haliç line) leaves Kadıköy at 11:00, 13:00, 15:00, 16:05 and 17:05, and reaches Fener 35 minutes later. There's also a 14:00, but only on Sundays and holidays.

The Pierre Loti cable car takes three minutes and runs from 08:00 to 23:00, but it stops in strong wind.

Taxis: use BiTaksi or Uber (which sends ordinary yellow taxis) and pay in the app. Avoid taxis hailed on the street in Sultanahmet. If a card machine offers to charge you in euros, always choose lira.

İstanbulkart: the city's tap card, which works on the trams, ferries, metro, buses and the Pierre Loti cable car. One card is enough for the four of us. We'll buy it from the yellow Biletmatik machine at the Sultanahmet tram stop (Gülhane, Sirkeci and Eminönü have them too). A card costs ₺165 and isn't refundable. The machines take Turkish lira notes only, no coins, euros or dollars, and anything extra you put in is loaded onto the card. Load about ₺2,000 to start and top up when it runs low, at any machine or at a kiosk with the İstanbulkart sign. The whole trip should come to about ₺3,000. One card can be tapped for several people: tap, wait a moment, tap again, once per person. On Sunday the two leaving by car in the morning hand the card to the other two. Ignore anyone at the machines who offers to help.

Fares (from July 2026) with an İstanbulkart: ₺46 on the tram and the cable car, ₺65 on the Karaköy–Kadıköy ferry, ₺59 on the Eminönü–Üsküdar ferry. Every tap is a full fare, because transfer discounts only apply to cards registered to Turkish residents. A contactless Visa or Mastercard also works at most gates, but costs ₺65 for the tram and ₺120 for a ferry, so the İstanbulkart pays for itself quickly. The tourist day passes (₺1,380 for three days) aren't worth it for the amount we'll travel.

Money: changing dollars, ATMs and cards

The rate this week is about ₺48.8 to the dollar. Anything around ₺48.5 or better for a $100 note is a good cash rate. Most things, including museums, restaurants and hotels, take a contactless Visa or Mastercard. Cash is for taxis, street food, small cafés, tips and topping up the İstanbulkart.

Changing dollars. The best rates in the city are at the exchange offices inside the Grand Bazaar, along Kalpakçılar Caddesi and by the Mahmutpaşa gate. There the gap between buying and selling is well under 1%. Merkez Döviz, by the Mahmutpaşa entrance, is often recommended. They're open Monday to Saturday until about 18:00 and closed on Sunday, so Friday afternoon, when we're in the bazaar anyway, is the time to change most of our money. The offices on the tourist streets of Sultanahmet are 2–5% worse, and the airport and hotels 7–12% worse.

  • Thursday: we don't need lira at the airport, because the driver takes dollars. After check-in, change one or two $100 notes at Divan Döviz on Divanyolu (open 08:00–23:00, three minutes from the hotel). That covers the İstanbulkart, the tram and Thursday's dinner.
  • Friday: change the rest at the Grand Bazaar. For the four of us, $300–500 in lira should cover what can't go on a card.
  • How: clean, undamaged, newer-series $100 notes get the best rate. Ask "how many lira for 100 dollars?" rather than trusting the board, which may show the rate for another currency or a sell rate. Walk away from "no commission" signs with poor rates. Count the lira at the counter and keep the receipt.

ATMs, as a backup. Use Halkbank, PTT (the post office) or ING machines, which charge foreign cards little or nothing. VakıfBank and TEB are cheap too. Avoid Euronet and YourCash machines and the private banks' ATMs in tourist spots, which can take 8–14%. Some prepaid business cards can't withdraw cash at all, so check before relying on one.

Always choose lira. When an ATM or a card machine offers to charge you in dollars or euros "at a guaranteed rate", decline it and choose TRY. Their conversion costs 3–12% more than your card's own.

Tips: 10–15% in restaurants (in cash if you can), round up taxi fares, and ₺50–100 for porters and housekeeping.

Souvenirs

Shop at the Grand Bazaar on Friday, our only chance, and at the Spice Bazaar on Sunday. Bargain in the Grand Bazaar, but not in the Spice Bazaar's fixed-price shops.

  • Baklava: from Güllüoğlu, vacuum-packed. Never at the airport, where the same box costs about double.
  • Turkish coffee: freshly ground from Mehmet Efendi, or with cardamom from Fazıl Bey. Light and cheap to carry.
  • Lokum: from Hafız Mustafa or the Spice Bazaar. The pistachio kind is best. It keeps for about three months.
  • Spices: sumac, pul biber, saffron and dried mint, from the streets around the Spice Bazaar.
  • Tea glasses: light and cheap, but pack them in the middle of the suitcase.
  • Peştemal towels: they weigh almost nothing, dry fast and get used every day.
  • Ceramics: buy what you love. Most are printed, not hand-painted.
  • Evil-eye beads: cheap, and easy to give away.

Buying tips.

  • Bargaining: chains and fixed-price shops don't bargain. In the Grand Bazaar, open at about half and expect to settle around 60–75% of the first price.
  • Pay in lira: when a card machine offers to charge you in dollars or pounds, say no and pay in TRY.
  • Tax-free: on bigger purchases in shops showing the Global Blue or Planet sign, ask for the tax-free form at the till, and get it stamped by customs at the airport before check-in. After fees you get back roughly 10–14%. Minimum spends vary by shop, so ask.
  • Gold and carpets: have gold weighed in front of you and ask for the gram price, purity and labour charge separately. For a carpet, get a stamped invoice (fatura) stating its age and origin; carpets over 100 years old can't be exported.
  • Packing: kolonya (Turkish cologne) is 80% alcohol, so big bottles go in checked bags.

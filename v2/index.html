Four days in Istanbul

Byzantine Constantinople and Ottoman Istanbul built on top of each other, two continents, and a lot of good food, from a hotel on the old Hippodrome.   • Late checkout and luggage storage for Sunday.
  • Saturday dinner at Akdeniz Hatay Sofrası (444 7 247), if we want the salt-crust chicken: order it at least two hours ahead; one serves two or three.
  • In our phones, not on this page: the hotel’s name and address in Turkish to show taxi drivers, the driver’s WhatsApp, and our flight details.
Getting aroundOne tram line does most of the work, and the ferries do the rest.

The İstanbulkart is the city’s tap card for trams, ferries, funiculars, buses and the Pierre Loti cable car, and one card does for all four of us: tap once per person, waiting a moment between taps. We buy it on Thursday at the yellow machine at the Sultanahmet tram stop: ₺165 for the card, lira notes only, and anything extra goes onto it. Load about ₺2,000 and top up at any machine; the trip should use about ₺3,000. On Sunday morning, the two leaving by car hand it to the other two. Fares: ₺46 a ride on the tram and the cable car, about ₺60–65 on a ferry. A contactless bank card also works at most gates, but costs more (₺65 a tram ride, ₺120 a ferry).

The T1 tram is our line: Kabataş, Tophane, Karaköy, Eminönü, Sirkeci, Gülhane, Sultanahmet, Çemberlitaş, Beyazıt and on to Fatih. Every few minutes from 06:00 to midnight; the Sultanahmet stop is five minutes from the hotel. Between 17:00 and 19:00 it’s packed. The T5 runs along the Golden Horn from Eminönü to Fener, Balat, Ayvansaray and Eyüp.

Ferries cross from Karaköy and Eminönü to Kadıköy and Üsküdar every 15–20 minutes. The Golden Horn ferry leaves Kadıköy at 11:00, 13:00, 15:00, 16:05 and 17:05 and reaches Fener 35 minutes later; check the day before in the Şehir Hatları app. Funiculars: the Tünel climbs from Karaköy to the foot of İstiklal, and the F1 links Taksim and Kabataş. The cable car to Pierre Loti runs 08:00–23:00, except in strong wind.

Taxis: BiTaksi or Uber, which sends ordinary yellow taxis, paid in the app. We avoid taxis hailed on the street in Sultanahmet.

Offline: the map and photos need a connection, so we open this page on the hotel Wi-Fi each morning. Emergencies: 112 for police, ambulance or fire.

MoneyChange dollars at the Grand Bazaar, pay by card where we can, and always choose lira.

The rate is about ₺48.8 to the dollar; anything around ₺48.5 or better for a $100 note is good. Museums, restaurants and most shops take contactless cards. Cash is for taxis, street food, small cafés, tips and the İstanbulkart.

Where to change. On Thursday, one or two $100 notes at Divan Döviz on Divanyolu (08:00–23:00), enough for the first day. On Friday, the rest at the exchange offices inside the Grand Bazaar, along Kalpakçılar Caddesi and by the Mahmutpaşa gate, where the rates are the best in the city (Monday–Saturday until about 18:00). For the four of us, $300–500 in lira should cover what a card can’t. Clean, newer $100 notes get the best rate. We ask “how many lira for 100 dollars?” rather than trusting the board, count the notes at the counter and keep the receipt. Offices on the tourist streets are 2–5% worse, the airport and hotels 7–12%.

ATMs as a backup: Halkbank, PTT (the post office) or ING charge foreign cards little or nothing. We avoid Euronet and YourCash machines, which can take 8–14%.

Always choose lira. When a machine offers to charge us in dollars “at a guaranteed rate”, we decline and pay in lira. Tips: 10–15% in restaurants, rounding up in taxis.

Tickets and bookingsOnly two things need booking: the boat and, if we like, Topkapı.

The Bosphorus boat. Direct with the operator on WhatsApp, for 17:45–19:45 from Karaköy and back, with the price and the pier confirmed in writing. Our first choice is Constantine Yachts (family-run since 1967, with its own pier in Karaköy and excellent word of mouth): a private boat from about €150 an hour, or the shared sunset trip at €34 each, free cancellation up to 24 hours; WhatsApp +90 536 399 14 42. Eureka Travel does a private boat from Karaköy for about €200, without a guide (+90 507 943 68 58). Bosphorus Yachts Karaköy runs a small shared yacht with a live guide, fruit and nuts (+90 540 292 24 87). We avoid anything leaving from Kuruçeşme, a long taxi ride away.

Topkapı. The official shop is millisaraylar.gov.tr (“Bilet Al”): ₺2,750 each, including the Harem and Hagia Irene, valid for one visit on any day within 90 days. The gate sells the same ticket by card; the queue builds after 09:30. Sites like topkapipalace.gen.tr are resellers.

Hagia Sophia. Free to pray, through the worshippers’ gate on the park side, which opens shortly before each call to prayer. The tourist route is the upper gallery only, 08:00–19:00 as last published (closed 12:00–14:30 on Fridays; we confirm at the hotel), €25 each, sold only at the entrance. The Blue Mosque is free; visitors go in between prayers, roughly 08:30–12:15, 13:45–16:30 and 17:30–18:30, and on Fridays from about 14:30.

At the door, no booking: the Basilica Cistern (₺1,950, card or İstanbulkart), Galata Tower (about €30, until 23:00, last tickets 22:00; also sold on muze.gov.tr), Chora (€20), Istanbul Modern (₺1,000), the Archaeology Museums (€15) and the Pierre Loti cable car (İstanbulkart). The MuseumPass (€105) doesn’t pay off for our plan.

Eating in IstanbulMeat in Türkiye is halal as a rule, so we can eat almost anywhere; we skip only bars, meyhanes and places built around alcohol or pork.

We like big flavours: döner, Yemeni mandi, desi food, a proper pizza or burger, steak and charcoal grills, and anything sweet. Two tips that help anywhere: ask for pul biber (chilli flakes) and acılı ezme (a hot chopped relish) by name. Both are free, and they wake up a plain plate. For the no-vegetables eater, sebzesiz means “without vegetables”, and a döner can always come sade, plain.

Lokantas

A lokanta is a canteen of home cooking, and the most everyday way to eat in Istanbul. There’s no menu: the day’s dishes sit in trays behind a glass counter, and we point. Say bir porsiyon (a portion) or yarım porsiyon (a half, to try more), add pilav, sit down, and pay at the till on the way out; about €25–45 for four. Go between 12:00 and 14:00, when everything’s fresh. Worth pointing at: kuzu tandır (slow-roasted lamb), tas kebabı (meat stewed in its gravy), etli nohut (chickpeas with lamb), hünkar beğendi (lamb on a smoky aubergine purée), mercimek çorbası (lentil soup), and sütlaç or kazandibi to finish. “Bu ne?” means “what’s this?”, and “Etli mi?” “does it have meat?”. Our picks: Aslan by the Grand Bazaar on Friday, Hacı Abdullah on Thursday’s option B, and Kanaat in Üsküdar.

Street food, day by day

Midye dolma (mussels stuffed with spiced rice) on Yüksek Kaldırım on Thursday; börek at Çemberlitaş and Maraş ice cream at Mado on Friday’s walk to the bazaar; a simit for Saturday’s ferry; lahmacun at Halil in Kadıköy; balık ekmek, the fish sandwich, done properly as a wrap at Yılmaz Usta on Sunday; and the garlicky wet burger on Taksim Square. Kumpir, the loaded baked potato, belongs to Ortaköy, which isn’t on our route.

The snack run

There’s no 7-Eleven here; the small supermarkets (ŞOK, A101 and BİM, with a ŞOK 200 m from the hotel) are where to try local brands. Algida and Golf ice creams, Ülker and Eti biscuits, Tadelle hazelnut bars, Uludağ Gazoz lemon soda, ayran, and şalgam (fermented carrot juice, the spicy acılı one) for the brave.

The originals

A few places started the thing they sell: Hacı Bekir for Turkish delight (1777), Kurukahveci Mehmet Efendi for packaged Turkish coffee (1871), Karaköy Güllüoğlu as the benchmark for baklava (1949), Kızılkayalar for the wet burger, and Baylan for the Kup Griye sundae (1954). Nearby institutions: Selim Usta for Sultanahmet köfte (1920), the Pudding Shop (1957), Vefa Bozacısı for boza (1876) and Pandeli (1901). İskender was born in Bursa in 1867; the family’s one Istanbul branch is in Nişantaşı, off our route.

Shopping and souvenirsThe Grand Bazaar on Friday is our one chance there; the Spice Bazaar is open on Sunday.
  • Baklava from Güllüoğlu, vacuum-packed; at the airport the same box costs about double.
  • Turkish coffee freshly ground at Mehmet Efendi, or with cardamom from Fazıl Bey, and a copper cezve and two small cups to make it at home. A double çaydanlık teapot goes with tea glasses.
  • Lokum from Hacı Bekir or Koska; the pistachio kind is best, and it keeps for three months.
  • For the kitchen: sumac, pul biber, isot (smoky Urfa chilli), saffron, dried mint, nar ekşisi (pomegranate molasses), tahini helva and Antep pistachios, from the streets around the Spice Bazaar.
  • Peştemal towels (Jennifer’s Hamam in the Arasta Bazaar is fixed-price), ebru marbled paper, hand-painted ceramics (most “İznik” is printed, so we buy what we love), and kolonya, Turkish cologne.
  • Turkish brands on İstiklal, open late on Thursday: Mavi jeans, Koton, LC Waikiki, and Paşabahçe glass.

Bargaining: in the Grand Bazaar, open at about half and settle around 60–75% of the first price; chains and fixed-price shops don’t bargain. Tax-free: in shops with the Global Blue or Planet sign, ask for the form on bigger purchases and have it stamped at the airport before check-in; after fees it returns roughly 10–14%. Gold and carpets: have gold weighed in front of us with the gram price, purity and labour listed separately; carpets need a stamped invoice stating age and origin, and those over 100 years old can’t be exported. Packing: kolonya is 80% alcohol, so big bottles go in checked bags.

Weather and packingDry and sunny: 19–25°C by day, 12–19°C at night, breezy on Sunday.

Late September is one of the best times to be here, but evenings are cooler than they look, and the boat and ferries are windy: Thursday at sunset on the water will be about 15°C.

  • Shoes that slip on and off easily (mosques, at least eight times) with soles for cobbles and hills.
  • A warm layer each for the water, and sunglasses.
  • Long trousers for the men and scarves for the women, for the mosques and Topkapı’s Relics chamber.
  • Passports and e-visas on paper and phone; both cards and some cash.
  • Type C plug adapters (two-pin plugs fit, three-pin don’t), eSIMs set up before landing, and room in the luggage for sweets.
Safety and what’s onBusiness as usual for visitors; the usual big-city care.

The UK’s travel advice (21 September) mentions protests outside Israeli diplomatic buildings and pickpocketing in tourist areas; if we meet a demonstration, we walk the other way. Türkiye play France in the Nations League on Friday night, but in Kocaeli, not Istanbul. The Culture Route Festival opens on the 26th, sometimes with free concerts at Galataport.

Common tricks: taxis with a “broken” meter (use the apps), the banknote swap (“you gave me ₺10”; pay by card), card machines charging in euros, bread or meze we didn’t order on the bill, the shoeshine man who drops his brush, friendly strangers with a cousin’s restaurant, and fake ticket websites. On İstiklal, keep phones in front pockets.
